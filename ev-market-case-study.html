The question

Two segments that look alike but are sold very differently

I started with one question: how should an OEM turn a strong e-bus delivery year into the next tender cycle, while building an e-LCV business that does not depend on government money? Answering it meant first separating two markets that headline EV numbers lump together.

  • Vahan counts registrations, not factory dispatches, so company "deliveries" differ.
  • Sources disagree on 2025 e-bus totals (4,441 vs 3,961). I used the later, revised series.

2. The tender-lag insight

E-bus market share is a lagging indicator of tender wins

The registration leaderboard says Switch is winning. But e-buses are leased per km on contracts awarded 18 to 24 months before the buses appear on the road. Today's share shows yesterday's tenders. To see tomorrow's leaders, you have to read today's tender results.

How a tender becomes market share

  1. Tender awardedCity or CESL picks the lowest per-km bidder
  2. Build and depot set-upBuses, chargers and power connections
  3. RegistrationBuses enter Vahan 18 to 24 months later
  4. Share shows upThe leaderboard finally reflects the win

Reading the past

Switch's rise from 3.6% to 28.2% is delivery of orders won in 2023 to 24, including a 950-bus contract. Tata's fall from 39.2% to 2.4% follows its step back from aggressive bids over contract and payment terms.

Reading the future

PMI and EKA won 80% of the 10,900-bus tender across Bengaluru, Delhi, Hyderabad, Ahmedabad and Surat. Switch, JBM, Tata and VECV got no allocation.

So what: expect 2027 to 28 registrations to shift toward PMI and EKA, regardless of who leads today. For a product or strategy team, the tender pipeline is the leading metric to track, not registration share.

3. The payback model

Why e-LCVs grow with no central subsidy

E-LCV buyers get no purchase subsidy, yet the segment grew 202%. A simple payback model on the EV price premium explains it: at typical urban use, energy savings repay the premium in about a year. Move the sliders to test it.

So what: daily kilometres decide everything. Fleets on fixed, high-use routes switch first; owner-drivers at 60 km a day wait. That points e-LCV makers at pricing and finance, not range.

4. Recommendations

What the winners will do by 2030

The winners will be OEMs that can operate buses cheaply on contract and sell e-LCVs through retail finance.

E-bus makers

  • Compete on cost per kmGCC pays per km, so a 2% uptime gap is lost revenue for 12 years.
  • Build or partner for an operating and financing armJBM's ₹750 crore raise and EKA's partner model show how.
  • Localise motors before April 2027It decides subsidy eligibility in the next tender round.

E-LCV makers

  • Price within ₹1.5 lakh of CNG and dieselKeeps payback near one year at typical urban use.
  • Partner with lenders, guarantee resaleFinance cost and resale fear are the main owner-driver barriers.
  • Build a second and third modelMahindra's single-model position shows the limit.

Policymakers

  • Plan the post-2028 bus programme nowPM E-DRIVE ends in March 2028 and tender pipelines need 18 to 24 months' notice.
  • Keep payment securityIt made GCC contracts bankable for operators and lenders.

For Switch Mobility

Profitable and leading, but starting FY27 with about 1,600 buses on order against 10,000+ at JBM.

  1. Refill the pipeline through PM-eBus Sewa, state tenders and private staff and intercity fleets.
  2. Bring cost per km down into the ₹40 to 66 band, after an ₹86/km midi bid was rejected in Bengaluru.
  3. Turn the e-LCV niche into a platform by keeping the cold-chain position and adding a lower-priced model.
  4. Use first-year profit to raise capital, as JBM did.

2030 scenarios, units a year

My base case is about 14,000 e-buses and 100,000 e-LCVs a year, roughly double and four times 2026.

Author's scenarios: e-LCV share of about 650k LCVs a year at 8%, 15% and 25%; about 21,000 e-buses already in central schemes.
